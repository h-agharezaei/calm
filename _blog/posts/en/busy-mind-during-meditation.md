If you can't stop thinking during meditation, nothing has gone wrong. Minds wander by default, and sitting quietly mostly makes that easier to see. You don't need to block your thoughts: notice that you've drifted, give it a short label such as "thinking" or "planning", and gently return to your breath. This guide covers the steps, the mistakes that make it harder, and how far the research goes.

## A busy mind isn't a failed meditation

Plenty of people try meditation a few times and decide "I can't do this, my mind won't shut up." Hidden in that sentence is the idea that meditating means having no thoughts. In the most common form, breath-focused meditation, thoughts are expected. The exercise is to notice your attention has left and bring it back.

One brain-imaging study followed that cycle in fourteen meditation practitioners. They focused on the breath and pressed a button each time they realized their mind had wandered, then returned. The researchers described four phases: mind wandering, awareness of it, shifting attention, and sustaining attention ([Hasenkamp et al., *NeuroImage*, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2011.07.008)). It's a small sample, so it can't tell us much about everyone, but the practical point stands: wandering wasn't outside the exercise, it was one of its phases.

So if you drift thirty times in five minutes and come back thirty times, you practiced thirty times.

## Why does your mind race when you sit down?

**Wandering is ordinary.** In a well-known study that prompted people by phone at random moments, their minds were on something other than what they were doing in about 47% of the samples ([Killingsworth & Gilbert, *Science*, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1192439)). Don't lean on the exact figure, though. Later work found that estimates ranged from roughly 10% to 60% depending on the answer options people were given ([Seli et al., *Consciousness and Cognition*, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2018.10.002)). Either way, everyone drifts.

**Quiet makes thoughts easier to notice.** This is a common observation rather than a research finding: once work and screens are out of the way, thoughts that were there all day become more visible. Your mind may not be any busier. You may just be watching it.

**Fighting thoughts can backfire.** A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that when people try not to think about something specific, the thought tends to come back more after they stop trying. A rebound *during* the effort showed up only when people were also mentally loaded with another task ([Wang et al., *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691619898795)). Those were lab tests of suppressing a chosen thought, not meditation, but they're a good reason not to make an empty mind your goal.

## How to label thoughts, step by step

Labeling (also called noting) means giving a short, neutral name to whatever took your attention.

1. **Sit and set a timer.** Five minutes is enough. Eyes closed, or a soft gaze at the floor.
2. **Find your anchor.** Notice where the breath is clearest: nose, chest or belly.
3. **Don't wait for a distraction.** Just follow the breath. Distraction will arrive on its own.
4. **Pause when you notice.** The moment you realize you were lost in thought, the key part of the practice has already happened.
5. **Add a simple label.** Silently and softly: "thinking", "planning", "remembering", "worrying", "hearing". Once is enough.
6. **Leave the story alone.** You don't need to work out where the thought came from or whether it's true.
7. **Come back.** Give the next breath your attention and carry on from there.

If hunting for the perfect word becomes a new train of thought, use "thinking" for everything.

### Does labeling actually work?

Direct evidence on labeling inside meditation is thin. Two nearby studies:

- In one imaging study, putting a name to the emotion in unpleasant images went with a smaller amygdala response than other ways of processing the images ([Lieberman et al., *Psychological Science*, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)). That's about pictures and brain activity, not thoughts mid-meditation.
- In an experiment with 147 healthy young adults split into four groups, the group that practiced "attention plus acceptance" for three days showed less mind wandering on a six-minute task than a group trained in attention alone ([Rahl et al., *Emotion*, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000250)). So how you meet a thought may matter, not only whether you spot it. It was a brief experiment and says little about daily life or the long term.

Treat labeling as a simple tool worth trying, not a proven way to quiet the mind.

## Coming back to the breath

You don't have to force your way back. A few things help:

- **Return to a sensation, not the word "breath".** Cool air at the nostrils or the rise of your belly gives attention somewhere to land.
- **One breath at a time.** Instead of "stay focused until the bell", follow just this exhale.
- **If the breath is hard, use a sound.** Some people find a steady sound easier. Each time a thought shows up, return to it.
- **If you're very restless, slow your breathing first.** A [two-minute breathing exercise](/en/blog/two-minute-breathing-pause/) can be a gentle way in.

## Common mistakes

- **Trying to empty your mind.** The goal is to see a thought and come back, not to have none.
- **Labeling with a verdict.** "More useless thinking" is a scolding, not a label. Pick a neutral word.
- **Analyzing the thought.** If it's important, tell yourself you'll write it down after the bell.
- **Grading the session.** A noisy sit isn't necessarily worse than a calm one.
- **Adding time to make up for it.** Doubling a busy session usually just tires you out.

## When to get help

A busy mind is normal, but not every thought should be left to meditation alone.

- **If practice makes you feel worse,** stop, open your eyes and notice your feet on the floor or the sounds around you. In a systematic review, about 8% of participants reported at least one unpleasant effect, such as anxiety or low mood, and the figure varied widely by type of study ([Farias et al., *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/acps.13225)).
- **If you have a history of trauma, severe anxiety or depression, OCD or another mental health condition,** talk to a psychiatrist or psychologist before practicing regularly. Keeping your eyes open or using sound as the anchor may feel easier, but check with them first.
- **If intrusive thoughts last for weeks,** disturb your sleep or get in the way of daily life, see a doctor or a qualified professional.
- **If you're thinking about harming yourself,** meditation is no substitute for urgent help. Call your local emergency number or a crisis line now, and tell someone you trust.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do I think more when I meditate?
You're probably not thinking more; you're noticing more. With fewer distractions, thoughts that were in the background all day become visible. Pushing them away may also make them return later.

### Do I need to clear my mind to meditate?
No. Breath-focused meditation doesn't ask you to empty your mind, only to return gently each time you notice you've drifted.

### What does it mean to label your thoughts?
It means silently using a short, neutral word like "thinking" or "worrying", then returning to the breath without following the story. It isn't for everyone; if it distracts you, skip the word and just come back.

### How many distractions are normal in one session?
There's no set number. Dozens in five minutes wouldn't be unusual, and no two sessions are alike.

### What if my thoughts are very upsetting?
Stop, open your eyes and take in your surroundings. If it keeps happening, talk to a professional, and if you have thoughts of harming yourself, get help right away.
