To do a 5-minute daily meditation, sit at the same time each day, set a timer, rest your attention on your breath and gently come back whenever your mind wanders. Five minutes is a realistic length for starting and building a habit, though research hasn't settled how much practice it takes to get any particular result.

## Is 5 minutes of meditation a day enough?

It's enough to get started, and that's mostly a practical point: five minutes is short enough to survive a busy day. It doesn't mean five minutes has been shown to produce a specific benefit.

The evidence on session length comes in pieces, and none of them settles the question:

- **One direct test.** In a study of 71 healthy adults with little meditation experience, four 5-minute sessions were compared with four 20-minute sessions over two weeks. The 5-minute group reported larger improvements in mindfulness and stress ([Strohmaier et al., *Mindfulness*, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-020-01512-5)). It was small and brief, with in-person sessions, so it doesn't show that shorter is always better.
- **One large review.** Across 203 trials of mindfulness programs, there was no robust link between the amount of practice and reductions in depression, anxiety or stress. Actual practice was often poorly recorded, so the review can't support causal conclusions ([Strohmaier, *Mindfulness*, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-020-01319-4)).
- **One caution.** In a trial with new meditators, 13 minutes of daily guided meditation was linked to some improvements in mood, attention and memory after 8 weeks, but not after 4 ([Basso et al., *Behavioural Brain Research*, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153464/)). It was also small and didn't test 5 minutes, but it's a good reason not to expect much from your first week.

## Pick a fixed time and place

Short sessions rarely fail because they're hard. They fail because you forget. So attach the practice to something you already do every day: after brushing your teeth, while the kettle boils, or before opening your laptop.

In a well-known habit study, 96 volunteers repeated a daily behavior of their choosing in a consistent context. Reaching 95% of peak automaticity took a median of 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254 ([Lally et al., *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)). That figure rests on only part of the sample, the upper range is a model projection since the study ran 12 weeks, and none of the behaviors was meditation. The practical takeaway is modest: if you still need a reminder after two weeks, nothing has gone wrong.

The same goes for location. A particular chair or corner of a room is enough. The point is one less decision to make.

## How to do a 5-minute meditation

1. **Set a timer.** Five minutes with an end bell, so you never check the clock.
2. **Sit comfortably.** Back upright but not stiff, shoulders loose. A chair is fine.
3. **Close your eyes,** or rest your gaze softly on a spot on the floor.
4. **Find your breath.** Notice where it's clearest: the tip of your nose, your chest or your belly.
5. **Watch it without steering it.** There's no need to breathe more deeply or slowly.
6. **When your mind wanders, come back.** Silently note "thinking" and return to the breath. This may happen many times, and it's part of the exercise.
7. **Finish slowly.** After the bell, stay for a few breaths before moving on.

## What to do when you miss a day

Just pick it up again the next day. In the Lally study, missing one opportunity didn't materially affect habit formation, although people who were very inconsistent didn't build the habit. So return at your usual time tomorrow, without doubling up to make up for it.

On a day with no room at all, do a tiny version: one minute, or three mindful breaths. The aim is to keep the link between that moment of the day and the practice. That's practical advice rather than a research finding.

## When should you meditate longer?

There's no scientific rule for this. One practical signal: you've practiced on most days for several weeks, and on most of them the bell seems to come too soon. Then add a minute or two and hold that for a week or two.

Staying at five minutes is also a perfectly reasonable choice. Going longer isn't a requirement, and if a longer session makes you start skipping, go back to what worked.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging a session by how calm you felt.** Some days your mind stays busy. Returning to the breath is the practice, not reaching a special state.
- **Going long too soon.** Jumping to 20 minutes after three good days can undo a habit that hasn't formed yet.
- **Practicing in bed when you're tired.** If you keep falling asleep and sleep isn't the goal, change your time or posture.
- **Switching techniques constantly.** Stay with one simple anchor for a few weeks. You can explore other methods later.
- **Expecting quick results.** Treat meditation as an attention skill, not an instant fix.

## The limits of the evidence, and safety notes

Meditation's benefits tend to be oversold. A review of 47 trials found that meditation programs produced small to moderate reductions in negative aspects of psychological stress and were no better than other active approaches such as exercise ([Goyal et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/)). Those were mostly structured, multi-week programs, so the results don't transfer directly to five minutes a day.

Meditation is low-risk for most people, but not risk-free. In a systematic review, an average of about 8% of participants reported at least one unpleasant experience, such as increased anxiety or low mood, with wide variation between studies ([Farias et al., *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/)).

- If you feel distressed, panicky or flooded by painful memories during practice, open your eyes, notice the sounds around you or your feet on the floor, and stop.
- If you have a history of trauma, severe anxiety or depression, or another mental health condition, talk to a psychiatrist or psychologist before you begin.
- Meditation is not a substitute for medical care, medication or professional advice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does 5 minutes of meditation a day do anything?
It's a sensible length for starting and building a habit. Direct evidence on five minutes is limited and one small study can't settle it, so no specific result can be promised.

### What's the best time for a 5-minute meditation?
Whichever time you can repeat every day. Tying it to something fixed, like brushing your teeth in the morning, makes it easier to remember.

### How long does it take to become a habit?
There's no fixed number. The median in the Lally study was 66 days, with wide variation between people and for behaviors other than meditation. Don't take the "21 days" rule too seriously.

### Can I meditate lying down?
Yes, especially if sitting is uncomfortable. If you keep falling asleep and sleep isn't the goal, try it sitting up.

### Do I need an app for this?
No. A simple timer is enough. You can also open Calm right in your browser; it's free and needs no sign-up.
