If you only have two minutes, slow your breathing down and make your exhale a little longer than usual. Three simple techniques do exactly that: coherent breathing (about 5.5 seconds in, 5.5 seconds out), long-exhale breathing (4 in, 8 out) and the physiological sigh (two inhales, then one long exhale). This guide walks through each one, when to pick which, and what the research can and can't tell you.

## When you only have two minutes

Most real breaks in a day are short: between two meetings, before replying to an upsetting message, in the car before you walk into the house. A 20-minute meditation isn't realistic in those moments, but two minutes usually is.

A short breathing exercise doesn't have a big job. It won't "erase" stress or flip your mood. What it can do is put a small gap between you and your autopilot reaction: a few slow breaths, your attention back in your body for a moment, and then back to what you were doing.

## Do quick breathing exercises work? What the research says

There's some evidence on slow breathing in general, but most of it comes from small studies:

- A systematic review of 15 studies found that breathing at fewer than 10 breaths per minute was linked to measurable changes in heart rhythm and to reports of greater relaxation, while noting that the mechanisms are still unclear ([Zaccaro et al., *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 2018](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353)).
- A meta-analysis of 12 randomized trials found a small-to-medium effect of breathwork on self-reported stress, but the samples were small and most studies had a moderate risk of bias ([Fincham et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)).
- In a remote randomized trial at Stanford, volunteers spent 5 minutes a day for a month on one of three breathing exercises or on mindfulness meditation. All groups reported mood improvements, and the "cyclic sighing" group (repeated physiological sighs) showed a larger gain in positive mood and a larger drop in breathing rate than the mindfulness group ([Balban et al., *Cell Reports Medicine*, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/)). There was no no-practice control group, mood was self-reported, and the daily dose was 5 minutes, not 2.

The "about six breaths a minute" behind coherent breathing comes from heart rate variability biofeedback research: at around that pace, the natural rise and fall of heart rate with each breath tends to be largest. Researchers in that field note that the ideal pace varies a little from person to person and that the proposed mechanisms are still hypotheses ([Lehrer & Gevirtz, *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756)).

The honest summary: no study has shown that two minutes of slow breathing has a reliable effect for everyone, or that one of these three techniques beats the others. They're simple, free ways to pause, not a treatment.

## Three 2-minute breathing techniques

### 1. Coherent breathing (5.5 in, 5.5 out)

1. Sit or stand upright and let your shoulders drop.
2. Breathe in quietly through your nose for about five and a half seconds.
3. Without pausing, breathe out for the same length.
4. Keep going for about 11 rounds, which is roughly two minutes.

The rhythm is smooth and even, with no breath holds. It's a good fit when you want to settle down but stay alert, like before a meeting or in the middle of work.

### 2. Long exhale (4 in, 8 out)

1. Breathe in gently through your nose for 4 seconds.
2. Breathe out slowly for 8 seconds, through your nose or through slightly pursed lips.
3. Repeat for about 10 rounds.

The exhale is twice as long as the inhale. If eight seconds feels like too much, start with 3 in and 6 out. Many people reach for this one when they feel more wound up and want to slow down. You can [open it in Calm with 10 rounds](https://tpis.ir/?breath=exhale&rounds=10&lang=en).

### 3. Physiological sigh (double inhale, long exhale)

1. Take a normal breath in through your nose.
2. Right away, top it up with a second, shorter inhale.
3. Breathe out slowly and fully, ideally through your mouth.
4. Three to five rounds is enough; then breathe normally for a few breaths.

This is the quickest of the three and works even when you have less than a minute, like right before you pick up the phone. Try [5 rounds of the physiological sigh in Calm](https://tpis.ir/?breath=sigh&rounds=5&lang=en).

## Which one should you use?

- **In the middle of work, want to stay calm and focused:** coherent breathing.
- **Feeling tense or irritated and want to slow down:** long exhale.
- **Less than a minute:** physiological sigh, three to five rounds.
- **You like breath holds:** [box breathing](/en/blog/box-breathing/) is another option.

This split is based on how the exercises are built, not on head-to-head research. The best approach is to try all three a few times and notice which one feels easiest for you.

## Common mistakes

- **Breathing too deeply.** Slow breathing means gentle, not filling your lungs to the limit. Very deep breaths can make you lightheaded.
- **Forcing the numbers.** If 5.5 or 8 seconds feels hard, shorten it. The ratio matters more than the exact count.
- **Too many sighs in a row.** A few rounds is plenty. Dozens of double inhales are more likely to make you dizzy than calm.
- **Expecting an instant change.** If you still feel tense after two minutes, you haven't failed. The goal is a pause, not a guaranteed state.
- **Only trying it at your worst moment.** A new technique is harder to use when you're very upset. Practice a few times when you're calm so it feels familiar.

## Safety notes

- If you feel dizzy, lightheaded, short of breath or notice your heart racing, stop and breathe normally.
- If you have a heart condition, asthma or another lung condition, or you're pregnant, talk to your doctor before starting regular breathing exercises.
- If focusing on your breath makes you anxious, or you've had panic attacks, start with fewer rounds and stop if it makes things worse.
- Don't close your eyes while driving; only practice in the car when it's parked.
- Breathing exercises are not a treatment for anxiety or any medical condition. If stress or anxiety lasts for weeks or gets in the way of daily life, see a doctor or a qualified professional.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can two minutes of breathing really make a difference?
For some people, a short pause is enough to go back to work feeling a bit steadier, but no study has shown that two minutes has a reliable effect for everyone. Most research looks at longer or daily practice.

### What's the difference between a physiological sigh and a normal sigh?
In a physiological sigh you deliberately add a second short inhale on top of the first, then make the exhale slow and long. A normal sigh is usually automatic and quick.

### Should I breathe through my nose or my mouth?
Inhaling through the nose is usually easier to keep slow and quiet. For the exhale, use whichever feels comfortable; slightly pursed lips make it easier to keep the exhale slow.

### Which technique is best for beginners?
Coherent breathing is the simplest place to start: no holds, and the inhale and exhale are the same length. Once it feels easy, try the other two.
