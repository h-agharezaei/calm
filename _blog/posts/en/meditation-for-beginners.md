To meditate as a beginner, sit comfortably for five minutes, rest your attention on one thing, such as your breath or a steady sound, and gently bring it back every time your mind wanders. That's it: no special cushion, no empty mind, no hour-long sessions. This guide explains what meditation is (and isn't), walks you through a first practice step by step, and is honest about what you can and can't expect from it.

## What is meditation (and what isn't it)?

Meditation is a broad name for a family of attention-training practices. In the most common form, you choose an *anchor*, like the feeling of your breath, and keep your attention there. Your mind will wander. Your job is to notice that and come back. Noticing and returning *is* the practice.

A few common misconceptions:

- **It's not about emptying your mind.** Thoughts will show up; that's part of the exercise, not a sign you're doing it wrong.
- **You don't have to sit cross-legged.** A chair, a sofa or lying down all work.
- **It doesn't have to be religious.** Many techniques come from spiritual traditions, but modern secular versions are open to anyone.
- **It won't always feel relaxing.** Some sessions are calm, others restless. Both count.

A wandering mind is the default, not a personal flaw. In a well-known study that used a phone app to ask more than 2,000 people what they were doing and thinking at random moments, people's minds were wandering in about 47% of the samples ([Killingsworth & Gilbert, *Science*, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1192439)).

## The main types of meditation

Researchers often group practices into two broad styles ([Lutz et al., *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)):

- **Focused attention:** you hold your attention on one chosen object (the breath, a sound, an image) and return to it whenever you drift. This is where most beginners start.
- **Open monitoring:** instead of a single anchor, you simply notice whatever arises (sounds, body sensations, thoughts) without chasing or pushing it away.

Some familiar techniques sit somewhere in between:

- **Breath awareness:** the simplest place to begin.
- **Sound meditation:** ocean or river sounds make a bigger anchor, and some people find them easier than the breath.
- **Body scan:** moving your attention slowly through the body. There's a 10-minute guided version in [Where do you feel emotions in your body?](/en/blog/emotions-in-the-body/)
- **Breathing exercises:** such as [box breathing](/en/blog/box-breathing/). Strictly speaking these aren't meditation, since you change your breathing on purpose, but a few rounds can make it easier to settle before you sit.

## How to meditate: your first 5-minute session

1. **Set a timer.** Five minutes with an end bell, so you're not checking the clock.
2. **Sit.** Back upright but not stiff, shoulders loose, hands resting on your legs. A chair is perfectly fine.
3. **Close your eyes,** or let your gaze rest softly on a spot on the floor a little ahead of you.
4. **Take three normal breaths** and notice where you feel them most: the tip of your nose, your chest or your belly. That spot is your anchor.
5. **Watch the breath, don't steer it.** There's no need to breathe deeply or slowly; just follow the sensation.
6. **When your mind wanders, label it.** Silently note "thinking", "planning" or "hearing", then return to the breath. Expect to do this dozens of times.
7. **Finish slowly.** When the bell rings, stay still for a moment, open your eyes and notice how you feel.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Fighting your thoughts.** The harder you try not to think, the louder thoughts get. Let them come and go.
- **Grading the session.** "That was a bad one" usually just means "my mind was busy", which means you got more practice at returning.
- **Starting too long.** Thirty minutes on day one often ends with giving up. Five minutes daily beats an hour once a week.
- **Controlling the breath.** In this practice you only observe. If you catch yourself slowing it down on purpose, let go.
- **Waiting for a special state.** Calm may come or it may not. Success is measured by returning, not by a feeling.

## What to expect: what the research actually says

Meditation's benefits are often overstated. A large systematic review of 47 controlled trials with about 3,500 participants found that mindfulness meditation programs produced small to moderate reductions in some negative aspects of psychological stress. It found insufficient evidence for effects on positive mood, attention or sleep, and meditation was not better than other active approaches such as exercise ([Goyal et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13018)).

Researchers in the field have also warned that "mindfulness" is defined inconsistently across studies, many trials are small or lack good comparison groups, and media coverage tends to outrun the data ([Van Dam et al., *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691617709589)).

A realistic view, then: treat meditation as an attention skill that gets easier with practice, not as a treatment. Claims like "life-changing in a week" or "a replacement for medication" aren't supported by the evidence.

## Safety notes

Meditation is low-risk for most people, but not risk-free. A systematic review found that, on average, around 8% of participants reported at least one unpleasant experience, such as increased anxiety or low mood, with wide variation between studies ([Farias et al., *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/)).

- If you feel distressed, panicky or flooded by painful memories during practice, open your eyes, notice the sounds around you or your feet on the floor, and stop.
- If you have a history of trauma, severe anxiety or depression, or another mental health condition, talk to a psychiatrist or psychologist before starting longer practices.
- Meditation is not a substitute for medical care, medication or professional advice.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a beginner meditate?
Five minutes is enough to start. After a few weeks of daily practice, if it feels comfortable, build up gradually to ten. Consistency matters more than length.

### My mind keeps wandering. Am I doing it wrong?
No. Everyone's mind wanders. Each time you notice and come back, you're doing exactly what the practice asks of you.

### What's the difference between meditation and mindfulness?
Mindfulness means paying non-judgmental attention to the present moment. Meditation is one way to train it, sitting still for a set time; you can also practice mindfulness during everyday activities like eating or walking.

### What's the best time of day to meditate?
Whatever time you can repeat every day. Linking it to an existing habit, such as right after brushing your teeth in the morning, makes it easier to remember.

### Do I need a meditation app?
No. A simple timer is enough. You can also open Calm right in your browser; it's free and needs no sign-up.
